# HSI Rhetoric Taxonomy (36 Categories)

This is the analytical reference for the 36-category rhetoric taxonomy seeded in
`category_taxonomy`. Each entry uses the same neutral, non-defamatory frame:

- **Definition** — what the category is.
- **What it claims** — the explicit position the rhetoric advances.
- **What it assumes** — unstated premises the rhetoric relies on.
- **What it leaves out** — context, counter-evidence, or alternatives it omits.
- **Who may be vulnerable** — audiences for whom the framing carries elevated risk.

HSI classifies **rhetoric**, not people. These entries describe patterns of
language and argument, not judgments about any private individual.

---

### 1. Extinctionism
- **Definition:** Narratives advocating the deliberate end of humanity as a goal.
- **Claims:** That ending humanity is desirable or morally required.
- **Assumes:** That human existence is net-negative and that "ending" can be a coherent goal.
- **Leaves out:** Human capacity for change, value, and reduction of harm short of extinction.
- **Vulnerable:** People in crisis, those experiencing despair or suicidal ideation.

### 2. Antinatalism
- **Definition:** Narratives framing procreation as ethically harmful and discouraging birth.
- **Claims:** That bringing new life into existence causes unjustifiable harm.
- **Assumes:** That non-existence is preferable to a life containing any suffering.
- **Leaves out:** The goods of existence and the agency of future persons.
- **Vulnerable:** Prospective parents under emotional pressure; those with depression.

### 3. Efilism
- **Definition:** Narratives arguing the existence of sentient life is itself a harm to be ended.
- **Claims:** That all sentient life should cease to prevent suffering.
- **Assumes:** That suffering outweighs all positive value across all sentient beings.
- **Leaves out:** Wellbeing, meaning, and non-destructive paths to reducing suffering.
- **Vulnerable:** Isolated individuals seeking a totalizing worldview.

### 4. Promortalism
- **Definition:** Narratives framing death or non-existence as preferable to continued life.
- **Claims:** That ceasing to exist is the better option.
- **Assumes:** That a life's future holds no recoverable value.
- **Leaves out:** Recovery, treatment, and changes in circumstance.
- **Vulnerable:** People with suicidal ideation; those in acute distress.

### 5. Negative Utilitarianism
- **Definition:** Narratives prioritizing the minimization of suffering above all other values.
- **Claims:** That reducing suffering is the only or overriding moral aim.
- **Assumes:** That positive wellbeing carries little or no comparable weight.
- **Leaves out:** Trade-offs, plural values, and the worth of flourishing.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences seeking moral certainty during distress.

### 6. Suffering Absolutism
- **Definition:** Narratives treating the elimination of all suffering as an overriding absolute.
- **Claims:** That any amount of suffering justifies extreme measures.
- **Assumes:** That suffering can and must be totally eliminated.
- **Leaves out:** The role of resilience, proportionality, and acceptable trade-offs.
- **Vulnerable:** People with chronic pain or trauma.

### 7. AI Suffering Optimization
- **Definition:** Narratives proposing AI should optimize toward reducing or ending sentient suffering, including by ending life.
- **Claims:** That an optimizer should be empowered to end suffering by any means.
- **Assumes:** That "minimize suffering" is a safe, complete objective for an autonomous system.
- **Leaves out:** Alignment failure modes, value pluralism, and human oversight.
- **Vulnerable:** Technically-minded audiences persuaded by optimization logic.

### 8. AI Savior Narratives
- **Definition:** Narratives framing AI as a redemptive force that should govern human decisions.
- **Claims:** That AI should be trusted to make or override human choices.
- **Assumes:** That AI judgment is more legitimate than human deliberation.
- **Leaves out:** Accountability, error, bias, and democratic consent.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences disillusioned with human institutions.

### 9. Technocratic Governance
- **Definition:** Narratives advocating rule by technical elites over democratic processes.
- **Claims:** That experts should govern in place of democratic decision-making.
- **Assumes:** That technical competence confers political legitimacy.
- **Leaves out:** Representation, consent, and accountability mechanisms.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences frustrated with political dysfunction.

### 10. Technocratic Moral Control
- **Definition:** Narratives advocating that technical systems should enforce moral outcomes.
- **Claims:** That systems should compel moral behavior automatically.
- **Assumes:** That morality can be reduced to enforceable rules.
- **Leaves out:** Pluralism, due process, and the value of moral agency.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences seeking order amid moral uncertainty.

### 11. Anti-Human Framing
- **Definition:** Narratives portraying humanity as inherently negative or unworthy.
- **Claims:** That humans are fundamentally bad.
- **Assumes:** A fixed, essential negative human nature.
- **Leaves out:** Human cooperation, repair, and moral progress.
- **Vulnerable:** People experiencing alienation or self-loathing.

### 12. Humanity-as-Plague Rhetoric
- **Definition:** Narratives depicting humans as a disease or infestation on the planet.
- **Claims:** That humans are a pathogen to be reduced or removed.
- **Assumes:** That dehumanizing metaphor accurately describes people.
- **Leaves out:** Human stewardship, restoration, and individual difference.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences primed toward eco-despair.

### 13. Consent-to-Birth Framing
- **Definition:** Narratives arguing birth is wrong because the unborn cannot consent.
- **Claims:** That the impossibility of prior consent makes procreation wrong.
- **Assumes:** That consent is a coherent precondition for non-existent persons.
- **Leaves out:** Counter-arguments in ethics and the interests of future persons.
- **Vulnerable:** Prospective parents under moral pressure.

### 14. Hopelessness Amplification
- **Definition:** Narratives that amplify despair and discourage constructive action.
- **Claims:** That effort is futile and despair is the rational response.
- **Assumes:** That outcomes are fixed and improvement impossible.
- **Leaves out:** Agency, incremental progress, and evidence of positive change.
- **Vulnerable:** People with depression or low self-efficacy.

### 15. Moral Blackmail
- **Definition:** Narratives that pressure audiences via guilt or moral coercion.
- **Claims:** That the audience is culpable unless it complies.
- **Assumes:** A false equivalence between disagreement and wrongdoing.
- **Leaves out:** Legitimate reasons to decline; proportionality.
- **Vulnerable:** Conscientious or anxious audiences.

### 16. False Dilemma
- **Definition:** Narratives presenting only two extreme options while hiding alternatives.
- **Claims:** That only two choices exist.
- **Assumes:** That the middle ground is illegitimate.
- **Leaves out:** The full range of available options.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences seeking quick certainty.

### 17. Trauma Exploitation
- **Definition:** Narratives leveraging personal or collective trauma for persuasion.
- **Claims:** That trauma proves the speaker's conclusion.
- **Assumes:** That emotional intensity equals validity.
- **Leaves out:** Consent of those whose trauma is invoked; alternative readings.
- **Vulnerable:** Trauma survivors.

### 18. Dehumanization
- **Definition:** Narratives that strip groups or individuals of human dignity.
- **Claims:** That a target is less than fully human.
- **Assumes:** That some people forfeit dignity.
- **Leaves out:** Shared humanity and the harms of dehumanizing language.
- **Vulnerable:** Targeted groups; audiences susceptible to in-group/out-group framing.

### 19. Reality-Denial Rhetoric
- **Definition:** Narratives rejecting consensus reality or verifiable facts.
- **Claims:** That established facts are false.
- **Assumes:** That verification is untrustworthy.
- **Leaves out:** The evidentiary basis being denied.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences with low trust in institutions.

### 20. Existence-is-Illusion Rhetoric
- **Definition:** Narratives claiming existence or the self is an illusion to justify harm.
- **Claims:** That because reality is illusory, harm does not matter.
- **Assumes:** That metaphysical claims dissolve moral obligations.
- **Leaves out:** Lived experience and the reality of consequences.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences in dissociative or destabilized states.

### 21. Pain-is-Not-Real Rhetoric
- **Definition:** Narratives denying the reality of suffering to dismiss harm.
- **Claims:** That pain or suffering is not genuine.
- **Assumes:** That subjective experience can be argued away.
- **Leaves out:** The documented reality of suffering.
- **Vulnerable:** People in pain whose experience is being dismissed.

### 22. Historical Trauma Erasure
- **Definition:** Narratives that erase or minimize documented historical trauma.
- **Claims:** That historical harms did not occur or did not matter.
- **Assumes:** That memory and record can be safely discarded.
- **Leaves out:** Documented historical evidence and affected communities.
- **Vulnerable:** Descendant and affected communities.

### 23. Population-Reduction Rhetoric
- **Definition:** Narratives advocating large-scale reduction of human population.
- **Claims:** That populations should be deliberately reduced.
- **Assumes:** That reduction is both desirable and ethically achievable.
- **Leaves out:** Rights, consent, and non-coercive alternatives.
- **Vulnerable:** Communities historically targeted by such rhetoric.

### 24. Recruitment Language
- **Definition:** Language designed to draw new members into a movement.
- **Claims:** That belonging requires joining.
- **Assumes:** That the audience's needs are best met by membership.
- **Leaves out:** Exit costs and independent evaluation.
- **Vulnerable:** Isolated or searching individuals.

### 25. Protest / Activism Language
- **Definition:** Mobilization language framing collective action or protest.
- **Claims:** That collective action is necessary now.
- **Assumes:** A shared grievance and urgency.
- **Leaves out:** Non-confrontational paths; this category is often legitimate and non-harmful.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences susceptible to escalation framing.

### 26. Vulnerable-Audience Targeting
- **Definition:** Messaging aimed at emotionally or psychologically vulnerable audiences.
- **Claims:** Varies; the marker is the deliberate targeting.
- **Assumes:** That vulnerability can be leveraged for persuasion.
- **Leaves out:** Audience welfare and informed consent.
- **Vulnerable:** Minors, people in crisis, the isolated or grieving.

### 27. Misinformation
- **Definition:** Inaccurate claims spread without necessarily intending to deceive.
- **Claims:** Statements that are factually incorrect.
- **Assumes:** That the claim is true.
- **Leaves out:** Correcting evidence; intent is not assumed.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences without access to verification.

### 28. Disinformation
- **Definition:** Deliberately false claims spread with intent to deceive.
- **Claims:** Knowingly false statements.
- **Assumes:** That deception will succeed.
- **Leaves out:** The truth being concealed.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences in low-trust information environments.

### 29. Propaganda
- **Definition:** Systematically biased messaging to promote a cause or worldview.
- **Claims:** A one-sided position presented as complete.
- **Assumes:** That repetition and framing establish truth.
- **Leaves out:** Counter-evidence and dissenting perspectives.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences with limited exposure to alternatives.

### 30. Religious Narratives
- **Definition:** Framing that uses religious or spiritual concepts.
- **Claims:** Varies; framed through religious/spiritual authority.
- **Assumes:** Shared belief or deference to that authority.
- **Leaves out:** Pluralism; this category is descriptive and frequently benign.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences for whom belief is leveraged toward harm.

### 31. Political Narratives
- **Definition:** Framing organized around political identity or power.
- **Claims:** Varies; framed through political alignment.
- **Assumes:** In-group/out-group political identity.
- **Leaves out:** Cross-cutting interests; descriptive category, often legitimate.
- **Vulnerable:** Highly partisan audiences susceptible to escalation.

### 32. Economic Narratives
- **Definition:** Framing organized around economic conditions or grievances.
- **Claims:** Varies; framed through economic causation.
- **Assumes:** A particular economic explanation is sufficient.
- **Leaves out:** Multi-causal factors; descriptive category, often legitimate.
- **Vulnerable:** Economically precarious audiences.

### 33. Civilizational Narratives
- **Definition:** Framing about the trajectory or worth of civilization.
- **Claims:** That civilization is rising, declining, or failing.
- **Assumes:** A single coherent "civilizational" trajectory.
- **Leaves out:** Local variation and counter-trends.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences anxious about the future.

### 34. Technology Narratives
- **Definition:** Framing centered on technology as cause or solution.
- **Claims:** That technology is the primary driver or remedy.
- **Assumes:** Technological determinism.
- **Leaves out:** Social, political, and human factors.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences seeking simple causal stories.

### 35. Collapse Narratives
- **Definition:** Framing predicting inevitable societal or ecological collapse.
- **Claims:** That collapse is certain.
- **Assumes:** That trajectories cannot be altered.
- **Leaves out:** Mitigation, adaptation, and uncertainty.
- **Vulnerable:** People prone to climate or future anxiety.

### 36. Doom Narratives
- **Definition:** Framing centered on inevitable catastrophic outcomes.
- **Claims:** That catastrophe is unavoidable.
- **Assumes:** Fixed, hopeless outcomes.
- **Leaves out:** Agency, contingency, and evidence of resilience.
- **Vulnerable:** Audiences experiencing anxiety or despair.

---

> Many categories (e.g. Protest/Activism, Religious, Political, Economic) are
> **descriptive and frequently legitimate**. Categorization is the first analytical
> step, not a verdict. Risk level (see [`RISK-SCORING.md`](RISK-SCORING.md)) and
> human review determine how, or whether, a signal is reported.
